How it works

From a mushroom to a tree, in four steps

No biology background needed.

Step 1

Read a DNA barcode

DNA is a long text written with four letters: A, C, G and T. Every species has its own version of the text.

A barcode is one short stretch that is easy to read and differs between species. For fungi the standard barcode is a region called ITS, a few hundred letters long.1

Researchers publish the barcodes they read in a public database, GenBank. This site uses one barcode per species from there.

Step 2

Line two barcodes up and count the differences

Two barcodes are placed one above the other so that matching letters line up. Then the positions where they differ are counted.

The share of positions that differ is the distance between the two species. Close relatives differ in few places. Distant ones differ in many.

Dark columns differ. A dash means one species has letters there that the other lacks. Each excerpt is 60 positions, chosen to be typical of the whole comparison.

Step 3

Turn the distances into a tree

Ten species give 45 distances. A method called neighbour joining2 turns them into a tree: it joins the two closest species into a group, then keeps joining the closest groups until everything is connected.

Step 4

Check it against the official classification

Biologists sort species into nested groups: genus, family, order, class, phylum. Each step up is a bigger and looser group.

The lowest group two species share shows how closely they are related. Below, shared groups span both columns. Where the columns split, the two species have gone separate ways.

Classification from the NCBI Taxonomy database.

Same family
Close relatives. About as close as a lion and a house cat.
Same order
Distant relatives. About as close as a cat and a dog.
Different orders
Not close at all. About as far apart as a cat and a mouse.
Limits

One barcode per species

If a database record is mislabelled, that species lands in the wrong place. Several records per species would catch this.

Best at short range

ITS is good at telling close relatives apart. Between very distant species it changes so much that the deepest forks of the tree are unreliable.

No confidence scores yet

The tree does not yet show how strongly the data supports each fork.

Labels

The edible, poisonous and inedible labels come from a hand-curated list with a source for each species. None are generated by AI. See the sources
