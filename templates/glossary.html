Glossary

The words, explained

The parts of a mushroom that experts look at, and the DNA terms used on this site.

A mushroom
Cap
The top of the mushroom. Its colour, size and texture change with age and weather, so the cap alone is a poor guide.
Gills
Thin blades under the cap where spores are made. How they join the stem, and their colour, matter for identification.
False gills
Blunt, forking ridges instead of true blades, as in chanterelles. Their lookalikes, such as the false chanterelle, have true gills.
Pores
A sponge of tiny tubes under the cap instead of gills, as in boletes.
Ring
A skirt on the stem. It is what is left of a thin veil that covered the gills while the mushroom was young.
Volva
A cup at the base of the stem, left by a veil that wrapped the whole young mushroom. It is a sign of Amanita, and it is easy to miss if the mushroom is cut instead of dug up.
Stem
Also called the stipe. Whether it is hollow, scaly, bruising or swollen at the base can separate lookalikes.
Spore print
The colour of the spores, seen by leaving a cap on paper for some hours. Two mushrooms that look the same can drop white and brown spores.
Mycelium
The main body of the fungus: a network of threads, called hyphae, in soil or wood. A mushroom is only its fruiting body, the part that spreads spores.
Mycorrhizal
Living in partnership with tree roots, trading nutrients for sugar. Chanterelles, boletes and Amanitas are mycorrhizal, so they grow near particular trees.
Saprotroph
A fungus that feeds on dead material such as fallen wood or leaves.
Lookalike
On this site, a species that iNaturalist observers have confused with another one, counted from identifications that were later corrected.
The barcode
Ribosomal DNA
Every cell makes proteins with ribosomes, and ribosomes are partly built from RNA. The ribosomal DNA holds the genes for three of these RNAs: 18S, 5.8S and 28S. The whole stretch is first copied into one long RNA, and the parts between the genes are then cut out.
18S, 5.8S and 28S genes
These genes build the ribosome, so almost any change breaks it. They have barely changed in hundreds of millions of years, which is why the 5.8S gene looks almost the same in every mushroom on this site.
ITS1 and ITS2
The internal transcribed spacers: the parts cut out and thrown away. They do no job afterwards, so changes in them are rarely harmful and build up quickly. That makes them differ between species, even close ones.
DNA barcode
A short stretch of DNA that is read to tell species apart. For fungi the official barcode is the ITS region: ITS1, the 5.8S gene and ITS2. Fast-changing spacers around a gene that never changes are easy to find and easy to compare.
GenBank accession
The ID of a DNA sequence in GenBank, the public DNA database run by NCBI. Anyone can look a sequence up by it.
Alignment
Lining two sequences up so that letters that came from the same ancestral letter sit above each other. A computer finds the line-up with the most matches and the fewest gaps.
Gap
A dash in an alignment, where one species has letters the other has lost or never had.
DNA difference
The share of aligned positions where two barcodes differ, counting gaps. 0% means identical barcodes.
Neighbour-joining tree
A tree built from DNA differences. It repeatedly joins the two species that are closest, after correcting for how fast each one changes. Read it from the right: the sooner two branches meet, the more similar the DNA.
Genus, family, order
Levels of classification, from close to distant. Species in the same genus are close relatives. Species in different orders split long ago.
